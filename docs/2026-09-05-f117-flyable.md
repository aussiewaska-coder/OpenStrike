# The Nighthawk flies

The aircraft control now walks F-22, F-117, Apache and round again. The two
jets share `jet_controller.gd` and differ in an `Airframe` profile.

## Where the coefficients went

`aero_model.gd` held twenty `const` coefficients, all of them the Raptor's, and
`flight_assist.gd`, `jet_controller.gd` and three test files reached for them
across 82 sites -- though only 19 of those are production code; 55 are in
`aero_model_test.gd` alone.

The formulas were never aircraft-specific. A lift slope, an induced-drag
factor, a wave-drag rise and a load limit are generic aerodynamics; only the
numbers fed to them belong to an aeroplane. So the numbers moved onto
`scripts/jet/airframe.gd` and `AeroModel` became something you construct with
one. `GRAVITY` stayed a constant. `flight_assist`'s functions had to stop being
static along with it, because one carried a default argument reading a
coefficient, which an instance cannot supply; that argument is now a sentinel
the body resolves.

`aero_model_test.gd` passing with not one numeric expectation altered is what
says the Raptor still flies exactly as it did.

## What the Nighthawk is

Its values scale the Raptor's tuned numbers by the ratio between the two real
aircraft, and each ratio is recorded beside the value it produced. They are not
measured F-117 coefficients, and `aero_model.gd` is equally explicit that the
Raptor's are not measured F-22 ones: the speed envelope is compressed to fit a
50 km theatre. What is preserved is the relationship.

|  | mush | corner | top | turn rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| F-22 | 44.7 | 134.2 | 260.0 m/s | 37.7 deg/s |
| F-117 | 54.1 | 132.6 | 129.0 m/s | 25.4 deg/s |

Half the top speed, a third of the acceleration, two thirds of the turn rate,
6 G rather than 9, and an afterburner that does nothing because the airframe
has none -- military and reheat thrust are equal, which makes it inert without
a branch anywhere in the input path. Thrust vectoring is zero, so both triggers
is an airbrake and nothing else.

It comes out lift-limited across its whole envelope: top speed sits just below
corner speed, so it never reaches the load limit and pulls 5.7 G at best
against a 6 G structure. That is true of the aeroplane, and it is asserted so
it stays true.

## Rigging a model the code was not written for

`_scale_to_reference()` read `bounds.size.z` as the wingspan. The Raptor's GLB
arrives with its span on Z, so that worked while it was the only aircraft. The
F-117's arrives span on X and length on Y -- 10.43 by 16.00 by 2.92 -- and the
same code would have read its 2.92 m height as a wingspan and built the
aeroplane about five times too large.

Each profile carries a basis that puts its model on the engine's convention
before anything is measured. The F-117's was found by trying every proper
rotation and keeping the one that measures 20.24 m long and 3.70 m tall against
a real 20.09 by 3.78, rather than by reasoning about which way round `Basis`
takes its arguments. The visual carries that rotation now, so the cockpit point
goes back through its inverse, and the cockpit camera takes its orientation
from the aircraft rather than from the visual -- identical for the Raptor,
whose visual only ever held a scale.

## The cockpit, and what is not in it

The model has no interior. Rendering from the seat shows the inside of the
canopy facets and the back of the exterior skin: no seat, no panel, no
consoles. The cockpit view is therefore the seat point, the aircraft's own
faceted canopy frame, and the helmet HUD that is already drawn for jet cockpit
views. Nothing was modelled to fill it.

Its canopy is opaque from the inside, which would have made that view a blue
wall. Its parts are all named `Object_N`, so neither `clarify_canopy()` nor
`_stow_landing_gear()`, which match on names, finds anything. Both now have a
sibling that works by position, in the model's own frame so that where the
aeroplane happens to be standing does not matter.

`jet_effects.gd` partitions the Raptor's trailing wing panels into ailerons and
hangs plumes off its nozzles. That is Raptor geometry, so the Nighthawk opts
out and has no moving control surfaces and no exhaust.

## Cost

The F-117 is a heavy asset: 107,757 triangles across 36 primitives and 33
materials, with 79 separate 1024 px textures. Imported at Godot's default
lossless mode those would hold 421 MB of VRAM. They import `compress/mode=2`,
so they hold 52.7 MB. This matters here more than it usually would, because the
same defect in the F-22's three 4096 px textures had been costing 256 MB.

## Still open

No weapons or bomber loadout. No moving control surfaces or exhaust. No
cockpit interior. The numbers are derived rather than measured and want a
device pass to confirm the aeroplane is unpleasant to fly in the intended way
rather than merely unpleasant. Suite: 86/86.
